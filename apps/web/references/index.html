Methods behind the map, its fields and the tides

  1. Booij, N., Ris, R. C. and Holthuijsen, L. H. (1999). A third-generation wave model for coastal regions: 1. Model description and validation. Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans 104(C4), 7649–7666. doi:10.1029/98JC02622

    SWAN, the wave model the NWS Nearshore Wave Prediction System runs; behind the waves and wind-against-tide layers.

  2. Chen, C., Beardsley, R. and Cowles, G. (2006). An Unstructured Grid, Finite-Volume Coastal Ocean Model (FVCOM) System. Oceanography 19(1), 78–89. doi:10.5670/oceanog.2006.92

    The FVCOM system and its coastal forecast applications, including NECOFS.

  3. Chen, C., Liu, H. and Beardsley, R. C. (2003). An Unstructured Grid, Finite-Volume, Three-Dimensional, Primitive Equations Ocean Model: Application to Coastal Ocean and Estuaries. Journal of Atmospheric and Oceanic Technology 20(1), 159–186. doi:10.1175/1520-0426(2003)020<0159:AUGFVT>2.0.CO;2

    FVCOM, the unstructured-grid model behind the currents layer.

  4. Chin, T. M., Vazquez-Cuervo, J. and Armstrong, E. M. (2017). A multi-scale high-resolution analysis of global sea surface temperature. Remote Sensing of Environment 200, 154–169. doi:10.1016/j.rse.2017.07.029

    The MUR sea surface temperature analysis (the SST layer).

  5. Codiga, D. L. (2011). Unified Tidal Analysis and Prediction Using the UTide Matlab Functions. Technical Report 2011-01, Graduate School of Oceanography, University of Rhode Island; used here through the Python port of UTide. Accessed 2026-10-16

    The harmonic fits behind the tide predictions at the USGS gauges, each to its own year of record, set on NOAA's footing.

  6. Dowell, D. C., Alexander, C. R., James, E. P. et al. (2022). The High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR): An Hourly Updating Convection-Allowing Forecast Model. Part I: Motivation and System Description. Weather and Forecasting 37(8), 1371–1395. doi:10.1175/WAF-D-21-0151.1

    HRRR, the model behind the wind layer, the next-48-hours plot and its forecast vintages.

  7. Hu, C., Lee, Z. and Franz, B. (2012). Chlorophyll a algorithms for oligotrophic oceans: A novel approach based on three‐band reflectance difference. Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans 117(C1). doi:10.1029/2011JC007395

    Background: chlorophyll-a retrieval from satellite ocean colour (a band-difference algorithm).

  8. Longuet-Higgins, M. S. and Stewart, R. W. (1961). The changes in amplitude of short gravity waves on steady non-uniform currents. Journal of Fluid Mechanics 10(4), 529–549. doi:10.1017/S0022112061000342

    How an opposing current shortens and heightens waves, and where it stops them: the basis of the wind-against-tide layer.

  9. NASA/JPL (2015). GHRSST Level 4 MUR Global Foundation Sea Surface Temperature Analysis (v4.1). NASA Physical Oceanography Distributed Active Archive Center. doi:10.5067/GHGMR-4FJ04

    The MUR SST dataset (v4.1), served through NOAA CoastWatch.

  10. U.S. Census Bureau (2024). TIGER/Line Shapefiles: Area Hydrography. Accessed 2026-10-16

    The shoreline the Tides view's outline is drawn from, smoothed (public domain).

  11. Wang, M., Son, S. and Harding, L. W. (2009). Retrieval of diffuse attenuation coefficient in the Chesapeake Bay and turbid ocean regions for satellite ocean color applications. Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans 114(C10). doi:10.1029/2009JC005286

    Background: retrieving the diffuse attenuation coefficient Kd(490) in turbid coastal and estuarine water.

Long Island Sound

The buoy observations at the heart of the Long Island Sound page come from LISICOS, the Long Island Sound Integrated Coastal Observing System at the University of Connecticut, and from the work of James O'Donnell and his colleagues there.

The observing system

  1. LISICOS, Department of Marine Sciences, University of Connecticut. Long Island Sound Integrated Coastal Observing System. Accessed 2026-10-11

    The buoys behind this page: temperature, salinity, oxygen, turbidity and weather.

Monitoring programs

  1. Connecticut Department of Energy and Environmental Protection. Long Island Sound Water Quality and Hypoxia Monitoring Program. Accessed 2026-10-11

    Sound-wide ship surveys since 1991: up to 48 stations every two weeks in summer, with nutrients and plankton.

  2. Interstate Environmental Commission. Western Long Island Sound hypoxia surveys. Accessed 2026-10-11

    Weekly summer surveys at 22 western-Sound stations: oxygen, turbidity, Secchi depth, and nutrients and chlorophyll at about half of them.

  3. Long Island Sound Partnership. Hypoxia: area and duration in Long Island Sound. Accessed 2026-10-11

    The EPA National Estuary Program for the Sound: the official hypoxia indicator, nitrogen targets, and how hypoxia forms.

  4. New York City Department of Environmental Protection. New York Harbor Water Quality Report (Harbor Survey). Accessed 2026-10-11

    Since 1909: fecal coliform bacteria, oxygen, chlorophyll and clarity in the harbor, including the East River and the western Sound's city waters.

  5. Save the Sound. How's the water: water quality monitoring programs. Accessed 2026-10-11

    Weekly bacteria (Enterococcus) monitoring at about 65 western-Sound sites, and the Unified Water Study of oxygen, chlorophyll and turbidity in the Sound's bays and harbors.

    Sparklines: the status table's lines and the outlook's 48-hour rows.

    The chart symbol for tide rips, eddies and overfalls that the wind-against-tide hatching follows.

  6. Thyng, K. M., Greene, C. A., Hetland, R. D. et al. (2016). True Colors of Oceanography: Guidelines for Effective and Accurate Colormap Selection. Oceanography 29(3), 9–13. doi:10.5670/oceanog.2016.66

    The cmocean colour maps the map layers follow: ordered ramps, the strongest value the most visible.
